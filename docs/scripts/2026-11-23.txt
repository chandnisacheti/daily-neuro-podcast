Nora: Story four of five.
Theo: A portable brain ultrasound system called Foqus just picked up FDA clearance for rapid stroke-risk screening.
Nora: San Diego-based Burl Concepts built the system, which scans the whole brain in about forty seconds to detect a patent foramen ovale—a small heart opening linked to unexplained strokes.
Theo: It listens for signals from tiny bubbles during a study, bypassing the need for a skilled operator to find a temporal bone window with Doppler.
Nora: My read is that putting this directly into outpatient cardiology clinics could completely eliminate the current diagnostic bottlenecks for stroke survivors.
Theo: The catch is that a 510(k) clearance means the FDA found the device substantially equivalent to one already legally on the market and is not proof that the test leads to better outcomes, so I want to see pivotal trial data comparing its sensitivity head-to-head with standard echocardiograms.
Nora: Story five of five.
Theo: A new preprint EEG study of fifty-four participants found that writing essays with ChatGPT showed the weakest brain connectivity compared to writing with a search engine or no outside tools.
Nora: Researchers split participants into three groups—ChatGPT, search engine, and no outside tool—and found that the AI group showed the weakest reported neural coordination and lower recall or quoting of their own text.
Theo: The signal here comes from scalp sensors tracking how different brain regions coordinate, though lower connectivity could just reflect a more efficient route rather than less effort.
Nora: My take is that when a chatbot produces most of the sentences, the writer has less reason to remember them, and the findings show that heavy AI help leaves the writer carrying away less of it.
Theo: Cool demo of EEG connectivity in a classroom setting, but until this gets peer-reviewed, I'm calling it an interesting association, not proof of permanent harm.
